# Instrument notes

## 1. The famous-rival question

**My rival is famous and my product is not, so the asymmetry is real.** RinseKit is a known name among Southern California surfers. It's sold at Costco, REI and surf shops, and you see them in beach parking lots. Its POD sells for roughly $95 to $140 depending on the retailer. PORTALO is a Kickstarter product nobody in the Torrance lot has heard of.

A respondent who recognizes Product B may arrive at Q10 with its shelf price already in their head. Their "most I would pay" for B is then partly memory rather than valuation, and nothing like that happens for A. Left alone, that would push WTP for B toward roughly $100, whatever the product is actually worth to them.

How I handled it:

- **No brand names anywhere.** Not in the descriptions, the image, the form title, the coach's email, or what I say in the lot. The products are labelled A and B.
- **Generic description built from specifications.** Product B is described as "a hard plastic water tank" that fills from a tap, with the same eight specification lines as Product A. None of RinseKit's own phrases are used.
- **Logo covered on the image.** That only goes so far. The tank's shape is distinctive, and someone who owns one will probably recognize it with the logo covered.
- **Q22 measures recognition instead of assuming it away.** Respondents say whether they own, have seen, or have never seen each product. In Week 12 I can check whether people who know B give WTP for B bunched near its shelf price, and estimate the system with and without them. If the two estimates differ a lot, that is a finding about how much the known brand carries.

What I can't fix: recognition happens in the respondent's head, and asking about it after valuation is the only place I can measure it without priming everyone else.

## 2. Describing both products evenly

The descriptions follow the same template. Each has a plain paragraph on how it works, eight specification lines in the same order with plain-English equivalents for every metric unit, three things it does not do, and what is in the box. Each gets one image with the same treatment: product only, plain background, marks removed, same size.

**I changed one line of my own description from survey one because it was no longer even.** Survey one said 3 bar was "roughly double the pressure of a typical household kitchen tap." Next to a product that runs on household tap pressure, that would tell respondents A sprays harder than B. It doesn't: 3 bar is about 44 psi, at or below what B stores from a typical outdoor tap (40 to 65 psi). Both pressures are now stated in psi and bar, and neither is compared to anything.

I gave each product the same number of limitations, and checked that none is dismissive. A's are about refilling, heat, and not rinsing a whole person. B's are about needing a tap, heat, and refilling. I also took out a size line I had drafted for B, because I don't have published dimensions for A, and one product with a size and the other without is uneven detail.

I searched both descriptions for comparison language: "unlike," "better," "more than," "only," "instead of," "compared." None appears. The two descriptions sit one after the other on the same page, and the comparison is left to the respondent.

**Order is not balanced, and I'm recording it.** A is always described first, valued first, and asked about first, because the assignment fixes the valuation order and Google Forms cannot randomize sections. If going first gives A an edge on appeal or WTP, it is the same edge for every respondent, so it shifts levels rather than the price effects. Still, it's there.

**Spray time for B is a range (3 to 4 minutes)** because the maker's current page lists 1.5 gallons while older listings give 1.75 gallons and "up to 4 minutes." I used the current capacity and a range that covers both versions rather than a precise number I can't source.

## 3. Identification

The four price conditions are exactly the prescribed ones, asked for A and then repeated for B, eight answers per respondent plus the two WTP answers. Condition 2 moves A's price with B's held at free. Condition 3 moves B's with A's held at free. Condition 4 moves both. Each respondent's own two WTP answers set the price levels. I did not add realistic price pairs, a grid, or a single scenario.

Two adaptations, both forced by the tools rather than chosen:

- **Yes/no wording.** My elicitation is yes/no, so "how many A per period" becomes "would you get one A in the next 12 months." I used "get" instead of "buy" because "would you buy it if it were free" reads as a trick question. The period is inside every one of the eight questions.
- **No answer piping.** Google Forms cannot insert a respondent's earlier answer into a later question. So instead of showing "$40," the questions say "your top price for A," which is defined on screen as the amount just typed. The price level is still each respondent's own WTP, which is what identification needs. To make it workable, all ten valuation questions are on one page, so both numbers stay visible while answering.

## 4. Read as a respondent

What I found reading the draft as a stranger in a parking lot, and what changed:

- **I'd lose track of my own numbers.** In the first draft the eight yes/no questions came on the page after the two prices, and I'd have had to go back to check what I typed for B. All ten are now on one page.
- **A zero made the questions nonsense.** If I typed 0 for A, "if A cost your top price for A" didn't mean anything. Added: "If you typed 0, that price is the same as free."
- **I wasn't sure I was allowed to want both.** In condition 1, both free, I wondered whether saying yes to A meant no to B. My first fix, "answer each question on its own," would have been worse: it invites people to ignore B's price when answering about A, which is exactly the cross-price effect I'm measuring. It now says each question is a different situation with both prices stated.
- **"Permanently free" sounds odd for something you keep for years.** I kept the prescribed wording and let "in the next 12 months" carry the meaning.
- **The repeat-respondent question named the product too vaguely.** Survey one was recruited as "a survey about outdoor gear," so that's how people remember it. Q2 uses that phrase and adds "that showed you a portable water sprayer" so people who did a different outdoor survey aren't screened out.
- **The pressure line favored A.** See section 2.

## 6. The Week 4 checklist, applied

The checklist is the one I used in peer-review-given.md, extended from six sections to seven.

1. **Sections present and in order?** Yes: screen, context, both descriptions, appeal, valuation, switching, characteristics. Valuation comes after description and appeal.
2. **Screener on the customer's decision, age gate first, "no" ends it?** Yes. Q1 is age and "No" submits the form. Q4 screens on who pays, the buyer's decision.
3. **Context oversells or promises the product will exist?** No. It says I'm comparing two products, makes no claim either will be available, and doesn't say answers decide anything.
4. **Could I judge each product's value from the description alone?** Yes for both. Each has capacity, weight, pressure, spray time, hose, settings, filling, power, limits, and box contents.
5. **Marketing copy surviving from the campaign or the rival's site?** None. I checked both. PORTALO's "one of the longest runtimes in its size class" and RinseKit's "hose to go" line are both out.
6. **Elicitation right for the product?** Yes/no for both. Both are durables bought once.
7. **Wording exact?** The four conditions follow the prescribed questions, adapted only from how-many to yes/no as the assignment allows.
8. **Unit and period inside the question?** Yes. "One that you would own" in Q9 and Q10. "One A" or "one B" and "in the next 12 months" in all eight condition questions.
9. **Zero easy to give?** Yes. The WTP fields accept 0 with no default and say zero is a normal answer. Every yes/no question has "No" as a full option.

## 7. What I cut for length

Two products, two WTP questions and eight condition questions make this longer than survey one, and respondents stop thinking when they tire. I cut survey one's "what could be done better?", the outing-frequency question, and "what do you currently do about sandy gear?" The switching question now gets at that in the respondent's own words. Like and dislike are merged into one optional box per product. Every remaining question is mapped to an analysis at the end of instrument.md.
