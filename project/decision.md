Decision

Product: PORTALO, a compact manually pressurized portable water sprayer
Campaign: https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/portalo/portable-pressurized-water-sprayer
Written from inside the venture, as the founder.

Part 1. The gate
1. What gets committed, and when

The purchase order for the second production run, 1,000 units, signed with the existing manufacturing partner in October 2026, immediately after the campaign closes on 1 October, for delivery in November and December. The tooling already exists, since a first batch was manufactured and sold at Concours Lepine, so this is not a decision about whether to build the product. It is a decision about how many units to commit to, and 1,000 is a deliberately conservative number. 755 of them are already owed to backers, which leaves roughly 245 units of genuinely speculative inventory to open a retail channel with.

That conservatism is not free, and it is why this decision has teeth. All 1,000 units come off the same run at the same landed cost, my working estimate being near $20 per unit, but a run of 1,000 costs materially more per unit than a run of five or ten thousand, which would land closer to $13. Committing at this size therefore locks in a high cost base, and a high cost base means the retail price cannot drift far below the campaign price and still leave a margin. The deposit is paid before anything ships. If the 245 speculative units do not sell, they sit in a warehouse at my expense and there is no second order.

2. Who the customer is

A parent standing in the Torrance Beach parking lot at 7:45 on a Tuesday morning while their kid's surf practice finishes, who is about to load a sandy, salt covered teenager and a dripping wetsuit into a car with cloth seats, and who keeps a folded beach towel permanently on the back seat because of it. There is no tap and no shower at that lot. Some of them keep a jug of water in the trunk. Most just live with the sand.

That is a narrower and more useful person than "a surfer," and it is deliberately the person I can physically find this week rather than the one the campaign's imagery depicts. It is also, I think, the better customer. The parent is the one who actually pays, the one who owns the car, and the one whose annoyance recurs on a fixed schedule. The surfer with their own wet wetsuit has the same problem from the other seat, and my instrument will screen for and report both, but the parent is who I am designing the question around.

3. What one unit is

One PORTALO: the bottle, the spray head, the retractable hose, and the strap, in one color. That is what a buyer pictures, a single object they clip to a bag and keep in the car. The campaign sells a two pack at 74 euros, but nobody imagines themselves buying two portable sprayers. The two pack is a bundling tactic aimed at households, not a unit of demand. My instrument will ask about one.

4. Over what period a buyer decides again

This is the weakest joint in the model and I would rather name it than paper over it. PORTALO is a durable good. A buyer decides once, and decides again only when they lose it, break it, or gift one, realistically once every two years. I will state the period as annual in the instrument, the data, and the cost figures, and treat a repeat rate of roughly 0.5 purchases per buyer per year as the working assumption. The risk is that respondents read "how many per year" as though this were a consumable and inflate the number, and the Week 4 instrument needs to guard against that explicitly.

5. Which unknown is blocking me, and is it demand?

Candidate unknowns, run through both filters.

Production consistency and fulfilment. Blocking? Yes. The company's own risk statement names leak testing, quality control, and customs delays as the live threats, and a bad first shipment to 755 backers ends the brand. Learnable through a demand experiment? No. This is learned from the factory, not from respondents. Rejected as the question, though it is arguably the larger business risk.

Regulatory. Not blocking. No approval stands between this product and a sale. The patent filing is worth noting only because it protects the design from copying. It has nothing to do with permission to sell.

Whether the product works. Not blocking. Eleven prototypes and a first production batch have settled it.

Whether anyone wants it. Not blocking. 755 backers and $50,722 against a $6,248 goal establishes that demand exists. What it establishes it for is a self selected international audience of crowdfunding enthusiasts who found the campaign because they were already looking for something like it.

Whether that demand survives outside the campaign, at roughly the campaign price. Blocking, for two reasons. A 1,000 unit run only covers its cost at a price near the $51 campaign price, so if buyers will not pay near $51 the unit economics never close. And the 245 speculative units are the entire retail launch, so if the price does not hold there is no retail channel to open and the venture ends once backers are fulfilled. Learnable, because a survey of people who have the problem but did not go looking for the product measures exactly this. This is my question.

Is it demand? Yes, but a specific slice of it. Not the existence of demand, but price adequate demand in a population that did not self select. One mismatch recorded honestly: if I were actually the founder in October 2026, the unknown keeping me up at night is fulfilment, not demand, and a demand experiment does not touch it. This course will answer the second most urgent question. That is worth knowing now and worth revisiting in December.

Part 2. The question
The failed version

How much would surfers pay for a portable pressurized water sprayer?

This fails the test. If the answer comes back "$60," I sign the production order. If it comes back "$20," I still sign the production order, because the question names no threshold, no population, and no commitment, so no answer forecloses anything. It is a topic. It would produce a number I could put on a slide and then act on in exactly the same way either way.

The rewrite

Would at least 10% of Southern California surfers, beachgoers and surf parents aged 18 and over in my reachable population buy one PORTALO at $51, the campaign price, enough to justify signing a 1,000 unit production order in October and opening a retail channel with the 245 units not owed to backers?

I set the threshold at the campaign price rather than below it deliberately. The campaign has already sold 755 units at roughly $51 to people who went looking for it. What I do not know is whether that price survives contact with people who did not. Testing a discount would answer an easier question than the one blocking me.

Favorable answer: 10% or more of screened respondents say they would buy at $51. Action: sign the 1,000 unit purchase order in October, fulfil the 755 backers, and launch the remaining 245 units into US retail at $51, then immediately reopen the run size question, because at that rate of take up 1,000 is too few and the third order should be substantially larger.

Unfavorable answer: fewer than 10% clear $51, and the price at which 10% do buy is closer to $30. Action: do not sign for 1,000. Order roughly 800, backer fulfilment plus breakage, open no retail channel, and spend the winter on one of two forks. Either take cost out of the product until $30 leaves a margin, or stop selling it to surfers and sell it as the travel and hygiene product the campaign page already leads with, where the comparison a buyer makes is against wet wipes rather than against a jug of water in the trunk.

Those are genuinely different Octobers. One signs a purchase order and starts a retail business. The other fulfils an obligation and reopens the positioning question.

Part 3. The stopping rule

I have enough when I have at least 60 completed responses from screened respondents in my reachable population and the share who would buy at $51 sits clearly on one side of my 10% threshold, at or above 15%, or at or below 5%, because a result outside that band settles what I sign in October and a result inside it would not.

Cost figures above are working assumptions to be replaced once landed cost is estimated in the Week 6 profit model. The 10% threshold and the $51 price are recorded here so that Week 8 can test whether they held.
