# Instrument notes

## Part 1. Elicitation choice

I am using yes/no demand, asking for the maximum a respondent would pay for one PORTALO.

The question is not whether the product is durable, it is whether a buyer takes one or several in a single decision period, and for PORTALO those two happen to agree but for a reason worth stating. My decision period, settled in Week 3, is one year. Within any one year, a person who wants a portable sprayer wants one of them. There is no version of this where somebody keeps a second in the garage as a spare, the way they might keep a second protein drink or a second pair of socks. The product is not consumed by use and nothing about owning one creates appetite for another.

The only case where several per period is even arguable is a household buying one per car or one per person, and the campaign does push at this with its two pack at 74 euros. I do not think that survives contact with how people actually decide. Nobody imagines themselves buying two portable sprayers. The two pack is a discount mechanic aimed at getting a higher basket from a buyer who has already decided to buy one, which is a pricing tactic rather than evidence of multi unit demand. If I treated it as how-many demand, the three anchors would produce a quantity line for a product where quantity is effectively fixed at one, and every number would look like precision I do not have.

The real weakness in my period choice is the opposite direction, and I flagged it in decision.md rather than hiding it here. One year is probably shorter than the true repurchase cycle, which is closer to two years or however long it takes to lose or break one. Stated as annual, my quantity figure will overstate. I am handling this by treating the repeat rate as roughly 0.5 purchases per buyer per year in the Week 6 model rather than 1, and by naming the period inside the valuation question so respondents are not answering about a subscription.

## Part 2. Read as a respondent

I read the whole form as a stranger being handed a phone in a parking lot. Six things I would have guessed at, misread, or answered to please, and what I changed.

**The image was the worst problem and I nearly missed it.** My first draft used the campaign's own hero photograph, which carries the PORTALO wordmark and the Kickstarter "Project We Love" badge. As a respondent I would have read the brand name, understood that this was a crowdfunding product, and had a price category in my head before reaching the valuation question. Worse, anyone curious could have searched the name and found the 44 euro tier mid survey. That is the exact anchor my whole Week 2 selection process was designed to avoid, and I had reintroduced it in the one place it does the most damage. I replaced it with a plain product photograph with all branding and badges removed.

**Metric units meant nothing to me.** 410 ml, 3 bar, 205 g, 60 cm. I would have skimmed all four and formed no picture, then priced the product off the photograph alone. I added a plain English equivalent to each one, and for pressure I gave a comparison rather than a conversion, since 3 bar is meaningless even converted. If the respondent cannot judge the specification, the specification is decoration and the valuation is a reaction to a picture.

**I would not have known whether shipping was included.** Reading "what is the most you would pay," I would have silently assumed a shelf price and mentally subtracted an unknown shipping cost, or not, with no way for me to tell which. Two respondents doing this differently produce two numbers that are not comparable. I added "including shipping" inside the question.

**The zero path felt like failing the survey.** My first draft said only "enter 0 if you would not buy one." Reading it as someone who genuinely would not buy this, that felt like admitting I had wasted the researcher's time, and I could see myself typing 10 to be nice. Since a curve missing its non buyers is wrong upward, and this is a product plenty of people have no use for, I rewrote it to say zero is a normal and useful answer and asked them not to round up to be polite.

**My screener originally asked about surfing, and that was too narrow.** It would have routed out the parent standing in the Torrance lot who does not surf but deals with the sand, and that person is my primary channel. I rewrote the screener to ask about time spent outdoors and about dealing with sand, salt, or wet gear either their own or someone else's, which catches both without telling the respondent which group I care about.

**"Who normally pays for it" was doing work I had not thought through.** I added it to screen on the customer's purchase decision rather than the founder's, which the assignment asks for. But reading it as a nineteen year old on a college surf team whose parents still buy their gear, I would have picked "it varies" and stayed in the survey. I decided to keep "it varies" in rather than route it out, because routing it out would lose real buyers, but I am recording here that I will look at how that group answers separately when the data comes back. If their maximums sit noticeably below everyone else's, that is the same problem the age gate exists to prevent, arriving through a different door.

## What I did not change, and why

I left the appeal section at four questions even though it is the longest block and adds nothing to the demand estimate. Reading the form straight through, the price question lands very differently after I have been asked what I like and what I would improve than it does cold. Without it I read the price question as haggling, and I would have understated. It is buying the honesty of the section after it and it stays.
