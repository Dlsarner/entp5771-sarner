# Peer review given

Instrument reviewed: Scoop, the supplied instrument in the Week 4 module. My assigned partner had not sent theirs by Friday.
Product: compostable dog waste bags on a refill roll with a clip on leash dispenser, sold as a monthly refill pack, crowdfunding at $14 for a three month pack.
Author's stated elicitation: how-many.

## The checklist

### 1. Are the six sections present and in order?

No. All six are present but the order is broken in the most damaging way available. The instrument runs Screen, Context, then "Section 3 — How much would you pay?", then "Section 4 — The product", then "Section 5 — What do you think?", then characteristics.

Valuation is sitting in position three. Both the description and the appeal section come after it. That means every respondent named a maximum price for a product they had not yet been shown, and then read the description afterwards. At the moment Q3 asks "What is the most you would ever pay for one refill roll?", the only things the respondent knows about Scoop come from the context blurb, which tells them it is "the world's first truly compostable dog waste system" and nothing else. They do not know how many bags are on a roll, how big the bags are, what the dispenser is made of, or what compostable means here.

This is not a section that can be moved after the data comes back. Every price in the dataset is a reaction to a sales sentence rather than to a product.

### 2. Does the screener exclude on the customer's purchase decision, and is there an age gate?

The first half is half right and the second half is missing entirely.

Q1, "Do you own a dog?", is a proper screener. It excludes people who cannot face the purchase decision, and it does it on the customer's side.

Q2 is not. "Would you consider investing in a sustainable pet care product?" screens on the founder's decision, not the buyer's. Nobody invests in a $14 pack of dog bags; they buy it or they do not. The word is imported from the author's side of the table. It also does two other things it should not. It primes sustainability before the product is described, so the appeal score later is partly measuring how the respondent feels about composting. And it ends the survey for anyone who says no, which throws away exactly the respondents whose price sensitivity would be most informative, since a dog owner indifferent to sustainability is still a dog owner who buys bags.

**There is no age gate.** Age appears as Q10 in Section 6, at the very end, as a characteristics question. That is not a gate. By the time a sixteen year old reaches Q10 they have answered everything, and their data exists in the file. The three conditions the checklist names are that the question is there, that it is first, and that "no" ends the survey. None of the three hold here.

This is the item where the fix takes thirty seconds and the consequence of missing it does not have a remedy afterwards, because the classroom exemption stops applying the moment a minor answers and approval cannot be granted retroactively. It has to be added before anything is fielded.

### 3. Does the context section oversell, or promise the product will exist?

Both, in three sentences.

"We're launching Scoop, the world's first truly compostable dog waste system" is a superlative and a first-in-the-world claim, in the section whose whole job is to be plain.

"Your answers will help us finalize pricing before we go to market next spring" promises the product will exist and names a date. A respondent who believes the thing is shipping in spring is answering a different question from one who understands it might never be built, and the first respondent has more reason to be generous.

"Thanks for helping out!" is fine and I would keep it.

### 4. Could I judge this product's value from the description alone?

No, and I tried. I have never seen this campaign, so I am the test case the checklist describes.

After reading Section 4 I still do not know how many bags are on a refill roll, which makes Q3 unanswerable in any considered way, because I am pricing an unknown quantity of an unknown thing. I do not know the bag dimensions, whether they are big enough for a large dog, what the dispenser is made of, how long the bags take to break down, whether they hold up in a wet bin, whether there is any compostability certification behind the claim, or what the refill cadence is. The product summary says it is sold as a monthly refill pack at $14 for three months, and none of that pricing structure appears anywhere in the instrument.

The description is about 60 words and roughly half of them are adjectives.

### 5. Is there marketing copy that survived from the campaign page?

Yes, and Section 4 is almost entirely made of it. Quoting:

"Scoop is a revolutionary approach to a problem every dog owner hates."
"no more guilt, no more plastic"
"The patented dispenser clips to any leash and never jams."
"Once you've tried Scoop, you'll never go back."

The last one is the worst of the four. It tells the respondent what their own future experience will be, immediately before asking them to rate appeal. "Never jams" is an unverifiable absolute, and "revolutionary" and "patented" are both doing persuasion rather than description. "A problem every dog owner hates" tells the respondent that they hate this, which is a thing the instrument is supposed to be finding out.

Strip all four and the section loses nothing factual, because there is almost nothing factual in it.

### 6. Is the elicitation right for the product?

Yes, and this is the one thing the instrument gets right. How-many is correct here. Dog waste bags are consumed by use, a household goes through multiple rolls in any reasonable period, and quantity genuinely varies by respondent in a way that a single yes/no maximum would throw away. Somebody with two large dogs buys several times what somebody with one small dog buys, and that variation is real demand information.

One thing undercuts it though, and it belongs to item 8 more than here: the instrument prices "one refill roll" while the product summary sells "a monthly refill pack" at "$14 for a three-month pack." Three different units in one document. Whichever one the author means, the same word has to appear in the product description and in all three anchors.

### 7. Is the wording exact?

No. The three anchors are present but they are in the wrong order, and out of order they do not produce a demand line.

The instrument asks:

Q3, "What is the most you would ever pay for one refill roll?"
Q4, "At that price, how many would you take?"
Q5, "If Scoop refills were permanently free, how many would you take?"

The order is maximum, then quantity at maximum, then free. The free anchor has to come first. It establishes appetite before price has entered the respondent's head, and asking it third means it is answered by somebody who has already committed to a number and is now anchored to their own previous two answers. A respondent who said $6 and "2" is not going to turn around and say they would take 12 for free, even if that is true, because it reads as contradicting themselves. Anchors 1 and 3 are supposed to bracket the respondent's own demand line, and here anchor 3 has been contaminated by anchors 1 and 2.

This one is worth being blunt about: it is not a style preference and it cannot be corrected in analysis. The number in the free column will be wrong and there is no way to tell by how much.

### 8. Are the unit and the period stated inside the question itself?

The unit is stated and the period is missing entirely.

Q3 says "one refill roll", so the unit is in the question, though as noted in item 6 it conflicts with the pack the product is actually sold as.

But Q4 asks "At that price, how many would you take?" and Q5 asks "how many would you take?" and neither says over what time. How many per week, per month, per year? A respondent thinking per month and a respondent thinking per year will type numbers that differ by a factor of twelve, and both will look identical in the CSV. There is no way to recover the intended period afterwards, and no way to tell which respondent meant which.

The checklist says the words have to be there, and they are not. Every quantity answer this instrument collects means something different from respondent to respondent.

### 9. Can a respondent easily answer zero?

No. This is the cleanest failure in the instrument.

Q4 and Q5 are both dropdowns reading "1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6+". Zero is not an option. A respondent who would not take one even if they were permanently free has no way to say so and must either pick 1 or abandon the survey. That is not a phrasing problem or a friction problem, it is a structural one: the instrument cannot record a non buyer.

Every dog owner who does not want compostable bags at any price will appear in this dataset as wanting at least one. The demand curve built from it will be wrong upward by exactly the share of the population that was never in the market, and nothing in the data will reveal that it happened.

Q3 is an open dollar field and would presumably accept 0, but that only makes the inconsistency sharper: a respondent can say they would pay nothing and then be forced to say they would take one anyway.

## Taking it as a respondent

I went through the whole thing answering honestly as myself, treating the dropdowns as live.

I do not own a dog, so Q1 routed me out immediately, which is the screener working correctly. I went back through as a dog owner to test the rest.

Where I would have guessed. Q3 stopped me for a while. I had no idea how many bags a "refill roll" holds, and I was being asked to price it before Section 4 existed for me. I eventually pictured a roll of about 20 bags because that is what I have seen in shops, and typed a number based on that. If the real roll holds 60, my answer is meaningless. I would guess most respondents do the same thing and none of them guess the same number.

Where I would have misread. Q4, "At that price, how many would you take?" I read this as how many I would buy in one shopping trip. Reading it again I think the author may have meant per month. I picked 2 meaning per trip. If it is scored as per month, my answer is wrong by however often I shop.

Where I would have answered to please. Q2 made me want to say yes. Saying no to "would you consider investing in a sustainable pet care product" felt like announcing I do not care about the environment, in a survey that had just told me it was about composting, and the survey ends if you say no, which I could see coming. I said yes. That means I am in the sample under slightly false pretences, and I think a lot of people would do the same.

Q20, "Are you concerned about environmental issues, 1 to 10", got an inflated answer from me for the same reason.

Q22, "Would you recommend Scoop to a friend?", I could not answer. I have never used it. Nobody who takes this survey has.

What reading did not show me. Section 6 is brutal in a way that is invisible on the page. It is thirteen questions arriving after the respondent has already done the work, and it asks for gender, household income, and ZIP code back to back. As a respondent I would have closed the tab around Q13. Taken together those three are also more identifying than anything this instrument needs, and the assignment says to collect no identifying information. Q15, breed, and Q16, how long you have owned a dog, do not feed any analysis I can imagine. If the author cannot name what each question segments, they are costing completions for nothing.

## The one thing

Rebuild the valuation block. It is one block and one edit, and it is currently wrong in four separate ways, all of which are in the category that cannot be repaired after the data comes back.

Concretely: move it so it comes after the description and the appeal section, reorder the three anchors so free comes first, add zero to both dropdowns, and put a period inside the wording of each question. It should read, in this order, "If Scoop refills were permanently free, how many would you take per month?", then "What is the most you would ever pay for one refill roll?", then "At that price, how many would you take per month?", with 0 as the first option in both dropdowns.

I am picking this over the age gate only because the age gate is a thirty second addition that the author will make the moment they read item 2, whereas the valuation block needs actual rewriting and reordering. Both have to happen before fielding. If the author has time for exactly one, do the age gate, because it is the one with consequences outside the dataset.

## Would I field this?

No.

Items 7, 8, and 9 are all wrong, and the checklist says any one of those four is grounds for no. The anchors are out of order, so the free number is contaminated. No period appears in any quantity question, so the answers are not comparable to each other. Zero is unavailable, so non buyers cannot be recorded and the curve is wrong upward by an unknown amount. Item 6 is the only one of the four that is right.

On top of that the valuation block sits before the product description, which means the prices are reactions to a marketing sentence rather than to a product, and there is no age gate, which is a problem the dataset cannot be cleaned of afterwards.

None of this is difficult to fix. All of it is impossible to fix later. That is the whole argument for not fielding it as written.
